Why a panel of narrow agents beats one clever prompt

14 October 20266 min read

The instinct is to write one long prompt that weighs every criterion at once. It demos beautifully and then quietly fails in production.

The model averages across concerns it should hold apart, and you lose any clear line on why a decision came out the way it did. For a system that has to be defended — to a reviewer, an auditor, or a user — that opacity is the whole problem, not a detail.

A single model asked to do everything hedges. A panel of narrow reviewers commits.

Splitting the work across one agent per rubric buys two things. Each reviewer stays inside a single frame of judgement, so its reasoning doesn't bleed across criteria. And every score arrives with its own trace, which means a decision can be read back line by line.

When the panel disagrees, the disagreement is the signal worth reading. A split between the evidence and impact reviewers tells you exactly where the case is thin — something a single blended score would have smoothed away.

None of this is free. You pay in orchestration and in tokens. But for any decision you'll have to stand behind, a trail of narrow judgements beats one confident paragraph you can't take apart.
